# AI Economics News Report — 2026-10-05

## Executive Summary

The labor-market thread dominates this cycle, and it is conflicted in an instructive way. BLS's September jobs report (+29,000 vs. ~84,000 expected; unemployment up to 4.2%; July and August revised down a combined 60,000, erasing much of August's "beat") landed alongside Challenger's September tally (43,281 cuts, fewest for a September since 2022, AI cited for only 3,961 or 9%). Neither is an "AI jobs apocalypse" print. The macro picture is a hiring freeze ("wait-and-see," per Challenger) with AI as one voice among energy costs, geopolitics, and rate fears, not a mass-layoff story.

The young-graduate question got three new empirical answers in nine days, and they disagree on the surface. Fairlie & Wu (NBER w35796 / CESifo 12994) find no statistically significant AI effect on recent-graduate *unemployment* in summer 2026 (7.3%, inside the 6.3–7.8% range of 2022–25). A Census Bureau working paper (CES-WP-26-56) and a Dallas Fed study (2026-09-22) find real harm to *employment and earnings* for graduates from the most AI-exposed majors: -5pp employment and -13% initial earnings in the Census paper. Fed Governor Cook's September 28 speech sided with the harm reading, saying she is "highly attentive" to a scenario where AI raises unemployment at least temporarily. These findings are reconcilable (different outcome variables), and the reconciliation is the story.

On money, the capex-and-capital machine is unbowed. Crunchbase counts Q3 venture at $159B, 64% to AI, with a record 27 billion-dollar rounds. Oracle reported RPO of $664B alongside negative $5B quarterly free cash flow. The FT reports OpenAI projects $278B of cumulative negative free cash flow through 2030 while seeking funding at $1.2T+, while Anthropic is reportedly profitable on an *adjusted* operating basis. Revenue is real at the frontier-lab layer; free cash flow remains the contested variable.

## Three studies, three answers on AI and recent college graduates

`labor` `displacement` `academic` `empirical` `us` · **Source:** [Fairlie & Wu, CESifo WP 12994 (also NBER w35796)](https://www.ifo.de/en/cesifo/publications/2026/working-paper/early-impacts-ai-employment-among-recent-college-graduates) · *Found: 2026-10-05*

**Tier 1 (academic/statistical agencies), mixed verdicts.** Fairlie & Wu use CPS microdata for June–August 2026: unemployment for 22–25-year-old recent graduates was 7.3%, inside the 6.3–7.8% range of the prior four summers, with no statistically significant relationship between occupational AI exposure and graduate unemployment. By contrast, the [Census Bureau's CES-WP-26-56](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html) (Orr, Tucker & Warren; administrative records, post-ChatGPT cohorts) finds the most AI-exposed majors saw employment fall 5pp and initial earnings fall 13%, about half from lower within-sector wages and half from sorting into lower-paying sectors such as restaurants and retail, with effects fading but persisting. The [Dallas Fed (2026-09-22)](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0922) uses Claude-based task exposure, Lightcast postings, and Texas administrative records in an event study: 10pp higher major-level exposure means -1.7pp employment probability within a year and ~5% lower wages among those employed; enrollment in exposed majors fell 4.8% per 10pp exposure.

Methodology matters: the null is on *unemployment rate* (cross-section, summer 2026); the harms are on *employment and earnings* with pre/post-ChatGPT event-study designs on administrative data. Unemployment misses people who take lower-paying jobs, go back to school, or leave the labor force.

**More:** [Washington Post on Fairlie-Wu](https://www.washingtonpost.com/education/2026/09/28/is-ai-killing-jobs-college-grads-not-yet-new-study-says/) · [Stanford "Canaries" update, covered 2026-09-14]

## Contrarian Take

### Three studies, three answers on AI and recent college graduates
The headline version ("not yet, new study says") rests on one outcome, the unemployment rate of 22–25-year-old graduates, and that metric is poorly suited to the question. The same-month Census administrative-records study finds the most AI-exposed majors with 5pp lower employment and 13% lower initial earnings, with half the earnings loss from graduates sorting into restaurants and retail (employed, so invisible to the unemployment rate). The Dallas Fed's event study finds ~1.7pp lower employment probability per 10pp of major-level exposure, plus falling enrollment in exposed majors, which is a *behavioral response that mechanically suppresses future unemployment counts*. Stanford's Canaries paper (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Tier 1) already shows a 19% employment gap for 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations. The defensible reading is "no unemployment spike, but measurable underemployment and wage scarring," and a Fed governor now appears to hold the same view. Counter-counter: Fairlie & Wu's null on exposure is cross-sectional with one summer of data, so neither side has a long-horizon causal design yet.
