# AI Economics News Report — 2026-08-17

## Executive Summary

The headline this cycle is the first hard look at whether AI capex is actually showing up in corporate earnings — and the answer, from Goldman Sachs's own Q2 2026 scorecard, is "not yet, and barely anyone can even measure it." Just 2% of S&P 500 companies quantified AI's effect on their Q2 results, and among that sliver, median earnings growth (17%) was statistically indistinguishable from the rest of the index (14%). The gains are real but concentrated almost entirely in infrastructure: hyperscalers and AI-capex beneficiaries posted 54% earnings growth — about half of the S&P 500's total earnings growth — while the corporate *adopters* of AI have nothing to show for it yet. This is the productivity-paradox debate's most concrete data point since the BLS TFP deceleration this report flagged in July, and it lands from a bank that has generally been in the optimist camp on AI capex.

Money and financing are the second theme. Goldman also quantified the AI-crowding-out question directly for the first time (a running worry since this report started tracking Fed systemic-risk commentary): of ~$600B in 2026 US AI investment, only about $50B is displacing other spending — smaller than the bears fear, but Goldman's own framing is that "AI's boost to GDP and the investment it displaces are both smaller than often thought," a subtler and less bullish claim than the crowding-out headline number alone suggests. Underneath that, the financing structure keeps shifting toward less transparent instruments: hyperscaler bond deals that drew 5x coverage in February drew under 2x by July, and Apollo/Blackstone built a $35B special-purpose vehicle in June to fund Anthropic's compute — debt that sits off Anthropic's own balance sheet. Labor-market data is genuinely mixed rather than clean-narrative this week: Challenger, Gray & Christmas's July report shows total layoffs falling to a two-year low even as AI remains the single most-cited reason for cuts for a fifth straight month — which cuts against both the "mass AI displacement is here" and "AI layoffs are a media myth" framings simultaneously. Finally, a new-to-this-report NBER theory paper on task-chaining automation (Demirer, Horton, Immorlica, Lucier, Shahidi) fills a real gap in this report's theoretical-modeling coverage, which has skewed heavily empirical to date.

## Goldman Sachs: AI Spending Is Surging, But Only 2% of S&P 500 Firms Can Show It in Earnings — and Those That Can Show No Real Edge

`productivity` `earnings` `investment-research` `data` `contrarian` · **Source:** [Goldman Sachs Research, via PYMNTS — Goldman Sachs Says AI Spending Not Boosting Corporate Earnings](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/goldman-sachs-says-ai-spending-not-boosting-corporate-earnings/) · *Found: 2026-08-16*

Goldman Sachs's Q2 2026 earnings-season analysis found that only 2% of S&P 500 companies quantified AI's effect on results at all, and of that small group, just 11% cited measurable productivity gains (mostly in software coding and customer support). Critically, the companies that did quantify AI gains posted median earnings growth of 17% versus 14% for companies that didn't — a small, statistically insignificant difference. Meanwhile, hyperscalers and other firms directly monetizing AI capital investment saw earnings surge 54%, accounting for roughly half of the S&P 500's total earnings growth this quarter. Read plainly: the AI earnings story right now is almost entirely a story about the companies *selling* AI infrastructure, not the companies *deploying* it — a sharp, bank-sourced (Tier 2) empirical complement to the BLS TFP deceleration and Goldman's own March 2026 finding of "no meaningful economy-wide AI-productivity relationship." This is the most direct evidence yet that the productivity-paradox thread deserves to gain ground this cycle.

## Goldman Quantifies AI Crowding-Out for the First Time: ~$50B of $600B in 2026 AI Investment Is Displacing Other Spending

`capex` `investment-research` `data` `us` `measured` · **Source:** [Goldman Sachs Research, via Axios — Quantifying the AI boom crowding-out effect](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/12/ai-boom-goldman-sachs) · *Found: 2026-08-12*

Goldman Sachs put the first real number on a question this report has tracked qualitatively since the Fed's Financial Stability Reports began flagging AI-linked systemic risk: how much is the AI buildout crowding out other investment? Goldman estimates 2026 US AI investment at nearly $600B (~2% of GDP) and incremental crowding-out at roughly $50B — modest relative to the total. The mechanisms are specific: AI token costs are displacing other corporate software/tech spending (a wash for GDP, since it just shifts spending around); data-center construction is pulling resources from other building activity (margins on data-center construction run more than double non-tech projects); and AI-driven borrowing has raised corporate borrowing costs by only about 0.05 percentage points, trimming perhaps $10B of non-AI investment. Goldman's actual conclusion undercuts both the strong-bull and strong-bear reads simultaneously: "AI's boost to GDP and the amount of other investment it displaces are smaller than often thought." Tier 2, methodologically transparent, and a useful corrective to crowding-out claims made without a number attached.

## AI Debt Is Migrating Off Balance Sheets: Apollo/Blackstone's $35B Anthropic SPV, and Hyperscaler Bond Coverage Falling From 5x to Under 2x

`capex` `investment-research` `report` `contrarian` `us` · **Source:** [Chicago Booth Review — How Worried Should We Be About AI Debt?](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/2026/august/how-worried-should-we-be-about-ai-debt) · *Found: 2026-08-14*

Hyperscaler corporate bond issuance exceeded $120B in 2025 (versus a ~$28B/year average 2020-2024) and first-half 2026 issuance already exceeds all of 2025 — but the more consequential shift, per this Chicago Booth analysis (Stefan Hepp), is that AI infrastructure financing is increasingly moving *off* the hyperscalers' own balance sheets. In June 2026, Apollo and Blackstone structured a $35B special-purpose vehicle to fund Anthropic's compute needs — the SPV buys chips and leases them back to Anthropic, keeping the debt off Anthropic's books entirely — a pattern of private-credit funds, insurers, pension funds and retail vehicles increasingly financing data centers that hyperscalers merely lease. Separately, demand signals are softening in the visible (on-balance-sheet) part of the market: hyperscaler bond deals that drew nearly 5x coverage in February 2026 drew under 2x by July, and Amazon had to sweeten a recent long-dated bond sale with 18-21bps of extra yield after orders came in at just 2.5x versus 3.2x in March. None of this is a crisis signal yet, but it's a direct continuation of the Fed's debt-funded-capex concern and the BoE's quantified bubble-tail-risk warning this report tracked last cycle — and the off-balance-sheet migration specifically makes the true scale of AI-linked leverage harder to see in any single institution's disclosures, which is exactly the kind of opacity regulators (the IMF's April GFSR, Senate Banking Committee letters to Treasury in January) have flagged as the risk that matters most.

## Challenger Report: Total Layoffs Fall to a 2-Year Low, But AI Remains the #1 Cited Reason for a 5th Straight Month

`labor` `layoffs` `data` `tech` `us` · **Source:** [Challenger, Gray & Christmas — Job Cut Announcement Report, July 2026](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Challenger-Report-July-2026.pdf) · *Found: 2026-08-07 (report covers July 2026 cuts; included as the authoritative Tier 3 source that resolves last cycle's flagged tension with lower-tier aggregator data)*

U.S. employers announced 33,429 job cuts in July 2026 — the lowest monthly total in two years — yet AI led all stated reasons for cuts for a fifth consecutive month, accounting for 10,970 of them. Tech remained the epicenter: the sector announced 9,867 cuts in July alone, bringing 2026 YTD tech cuts to 149,023, up 67% from the same point in 2025. Andy Challenger's framing: "AI is shifting the labor market, it is not dismantling it" — hiring is actually up 25% year-over-year even as AI-cited cuts continue. This is the primary-source data (Challenger is the actual tracker; last cycle's report relied on a lower-tier secondary aggregator claiming YTD cuts had "surpassed all of 2025") that should replace, not supplement, last week's noisier layoff-tracker figure — and it lands the same complicated way the Fed's Beige Book has all summer: AI is real and specifically named, the aggregate labor market is not collapsing, and the two facts coexist. Context: this arrives against a July 2026 BLS Employment Situation report (nonfarm payrolls -23,000, unemployment 4.1%, with May/June revised down a combined 103,000) that shows broader labor-market softness not obviously AI-attributable — a reminder that AI-cited layoff counts and macro labor weakness are two different instruments measuring different things.

## New NBER Theory Paper: AI Automation Spreads Through "Task Chains," Not Uniformly Across Jobs

`productivity` `automation` `academic` `nber` `augmentation` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 34859 — Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work: A Theory of AI Automation (Demirer, Horton, Immorlica, Lucier, Shahidi)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34859) · *Found: 2026-08-17 (paper dated February 2026; included to fill this report's thin theoretical-modeling coverage relative to its empirical coverage)*

This MIT/Microsoft Research collaboration (Demirer, Horton, Immorlica, Lucier, Shahidi) models production as a sequence of discrete steps, each of which can be done manually, AI-augmented, or fully AI-automated — and finds empirically that AI-executed steps cluster into contiguous "chains" within jobs rather than being scattered evenly across a job's tasks. The model's key predictions, borne out in their data: AI-executed steps co-occur in chains; jobs where AI-exposed steps are more dispersed see *lower* overall AI execution (because coordination costs eat the specialization gains); and a step adjacent to an already-AI-executed step is more likely to become AI-executed itself. The macro implication is that AI-driven productivity gains should be non-linear — small at first as isolated steps get automated, then accelerating once enough adjacent steps chain together to eliminate a job's coordination overhead entirely. This is a genuinely useful theoretical complement to the aggregation puzzle this report has flagged repeatedly (why don't task-level RCT productivity gains show up in nonfarm TFP yet): if chaining is the real mechanism, current AI deployment may simply not have reached critical mass within most jobs yet, which is a testable, falsifiable claim rather than a hand-wave.

**More:** [SSRN — Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6290352) · [Microsoft Research — Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/chaining-tasks-redefining-work-a-theory-of-ai-automation/)

## Market Structure Note: AI Melt-Up Continues, But Wall Street Is Watching Rates, Not Earnings, as the Real Risk

`valuation` `capex` `investment-research` `data` `us` · **Source:** [Bloomberg — At AI-Fueled Market Party, Wall Street Eyes the Rates Punch Bowl](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-16/at-ai-fueled-market-party-wall-street-eyes-the-rates-punch-bowl) · *Found: 2026-08-16*

Big Tech's AI capex enthusiasm continues pushing US equities to fresh records (S&P 500 +13.4% YTD), but Bloomberg frames the real near-term risk as interest-rate sensitivity, not the earnings-quantification gap Goldman just documented: Alphabet, Amazon and Meta collectively hold roughly $80B in bonds maturing in 2056 or later, and the group (with Microsoft) is projected to spend a combined $740B on AI compute infrastructure in 2026 and another $1 trillion in 2027. Because AI capex returns are back-loaded by years, these names' valuations are unusually duration-sensitive — a rate shock would hit the AI trade harder than most of the market. Read alongside this cycle's other items: the market is pricing a long-duration bet on AI payoff at the exact moment Goldman's own earnings data shows that payoff hasn't materialized broadly yet, and the financing backing it is quietly moving into less transparent, more rate-sensitive structures (the Apollo/Blackstone SPV above). Tier 3 market commentary, but the underlying capex and bond-maturity figures are hard company data.
