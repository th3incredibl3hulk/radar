# AI Economics News Report — 2026-07-13

## Executive Summary

This is a short cycle — only two days since the inaugural report (2026-07-11) — but it landed a genuinely significant academic development and a cluster of labor-market data that meaningfully updates last cycle's picture. David Autor has joined Daron Acemoglu (with Simon Johnson) on a new NBER paper, "Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence," which reframes the whole debate: instead of asking "how much productivity growth," it asks "productivity growth captured by whom," arguing AI's collaborative, augmenting potential is being left on the table because of misaligned incentives, path dependence, and pro-automation ideology in how the technology gets built — a normative, policy-design argument from two of the field's most-cited empiricists, not just another magnitude estimate.

On labor displacement, the aggregate data keeps cutting against the "fast severe displacement" camp: a new Federal Reserve Notes study (Census BTOS + Lightcast job postings, Sept 2023–Nov 2025) finds **no evidence of reduced job postings** in AI-adopting industries or firms, and a Stanford/SSRN paper (Hartley, Jolevski, Melo, Moore) using the same window finds small **positive** wage effects and no significant employment decline despite generative AI usage rocketing from 30.1% to 35.9% of US workers in a year. But — and this is the load-bearing nuance — Indeed Hiring Lab's newest data shows the *composition* of the labor market shifting hard: AI-exposed occupations flipped from the steepest job-posting declines (2022–2025) to leading the recovery from May 2025 onward, and 71% of that recovery is senior roles. Read together: AI isn't shrinking the aggregate job pool, but it is re-sorting who gets hired within it — reinforcing "distribution matters more than magnitude" as the strongest empirically-supported thesis right now.

On money, PitchBook finally supplies the valuation-premium data flagged as a gap last cycle: AI startups carry a roughly 4x pre-money valuation premium over non-AI peers at Series D+ ($4.7B vs. $1.3B median), and an 84% premium even at Series A — the froth is structural, not just a mega-round story. JPMorgan pushed back on the bubble-skeptic camp with real numbers (hyperscaler cloud AI revenue growing 28–123% YoY across AWS/Google Cloud/Azure), a useful counterweight to last cycle's Woo/Yardeni split. Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index Economy chapter supplies the cleanest task-level productivity range yet — 14–50% depending on task type, shrinking for reasoning-heavy work — while confirming the same 22–25-year-old software-developer employment decline (~20%) that's now showing up across three independent sources this cycle.

## Acemoglu and Autor Join Forces on "Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence"

`labor` `productivity` `academic` `nber` `paper` `contrarian` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 34854 — Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, and Simon Johnson (all MIT) published a new NBER paper defining "pro-worker technologies" as those that make human skills more valuable by expanding worker capabilities — and arguing AI is currently being built and deployed in ways that squander this potential. They lay out five categories of technological change (labor-augmenting, capital-augmenting, automating, expertise-leveling, new-task-creating) and argue only the last is unambiguously good for workers under current conditions. Their diagnosis: it's not that AI *can't* be collaborative and augmenting, it's that misaligned incentives between firms and AI developers, path dependence, and a pro-automation ideology in the industry are steering deployment toward pure substitution. Nine policy recommendations follow, including targeted healthcare/education investment, tax reform, antitrust enforcement, and IP protections for worker expertise. This is significant less for new numbers than for who's saying it: Autor hadn't produced a distinct 2026 finding through last cycle, and his direct collaboration with Acemoglu on a *normative* framework (not just another magnitude estimate) elevates "distribution/design of gains" from a side debate to the paper's central thesis.

**More:** [MIT Economics — paper PDF](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Building%20Pro-Worker%20Artificial%20Intelligence.pdf)

## Fed and Stanford Studies Converge: No Aggregate Job-Posting or Employment Decline From AI — Yet

`labor` `displacement` `empirical` `academic` `contrarian` · **Source:** [Federal Reserve — AI Adoption and Firms' Job-Posting Behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/ai-adoption-and-firms-job-posting-behavior-20260327.html) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

A Fed Notes study combining Lightcast job-postings data (65,000+ sources, Sept 2023–Nov 2025) with the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (~1.2M businesses, fielded every 12 weeks) finds "no evidence of a reduction in job postings for industries or firms which have higher levels of AI adoption" — effects are neutral to slightly positive, and firm-level positive effects decay to negligible within 12 months. Independently, Hartley, Jolevski, Melo, and Moore's SSRN paper (Stanford-affiliated) finds generative AI usage among US workers rocketed from 30.1% (Dec 2024) to 35.9% (Dec 2025), yet wage effects are small and *positive*, with no statistically significant employment or job-opening decline in exposed occupations — concentrated instead among younger, college-educated, higher earners. Both explicitly caveat this is backward-looking; neither predicts future impact. This is the strongest aggregate evidence yet against the "fast severe displacement" camp, using two independent, non-overlapping data sources arriving at the same conclusion in the same window.

**More:** [SSRN — The Labor Market Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5136877)

## Indeed Hiring Lab: AI-Exposed Occupations Flip From Steepest Declines to Leading the Recovery — But It's a Senior-Only Recovery

`labor` `displacement` `augmentation` `empirical` `report` `contrarian` · **Source:** [Indeed Hiring Lab — AI and Job Postings: From Destruction to Creation?](https://www.hiringlab.org/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) · *Found: 2026-07-08*

Fills a gap flagged last cycle. Between 2022 and early 2025, AI-exposed occupations saw the steepest job-posting declines of any category. From May 2025 onward — after Claude Code's launch — that relationship reversed: more AI-exposed occupations are now leading the *recovery*, and US software-development postings have grown nearly 15% even as overall postings fell 7%. But the rebound is narrow and skewed: 71% of the software-development increase is senior-level positions, and 37% explicitly names AI in the job title — this is a "seniority-biased technological change" story, not a broad rebound. Software postings remain ~27.5% below pre-pandemic levels even after the recovery. English-speaking countries with higher agentic-AI adoption show stronger recoveries than non-English-speaking peers. This directly complicates last cycle's Fed-driven "weak hiring, not AI" youth-unemployment framing: AI-exposed roles are recovering, but the recovery is capturing experienced workers, not the entry-level cohort that was hit first.

## Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index: Task-Level Productivity Gains Range 14–50%, Shrink With Reasoning Complexity

`productivity` `funding` `empirical` `report` `academic` · **Source:** [Stanford HAI — 2026 AI Index Report, Economy Chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

Stanford HAI's flagship annual index (Tier 1 aggregator, though it compiles rather than generates primary research) supplies the cleanest task-level productivity range surfaced yet: customer support +14–15%, software development +26%, marketing output +50% — with the report explicitly noting gains shrink for work requiring deeper reasoning. On investment: private AI investment grew 127.5% in 2025 (generative AI alone >200%), the US committed roughly 23x China's private AI investment, and Google alone reported >$150B in 2025 capex. On adoption: 88% organizational adoption, generative AI hit 70% business-function penetration in three years (faster than PCs or the internet), but agent deployment remains in the single digits across most functions — consistent with McKinsey's scaling-gap finding from last cycle. On labor: employment for software developers aged 22–25 fell nearly 20% from 2024, and a third of organizations anticipate workforce reductions over the coming year — but the report is careful to flag these as *anticipated*, not yet observed in aggregate employment data, echoing the Fed/Stanford convergence above.

## PIIE: "Research on AI and the Labor Market Is Still in the First Inning" — A Credible Voice Urges Caution on Both Sides

`labor` `academic` `opinion` `contrarian` `measured` · **Source:** [PIIE — Research on AI and the labor market is still in the first inning](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/research-ai-and-labor-market-still-first-inning) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

Jed Kolko (PIIE, Tier 2) synthesizes the genuinely contradictory state of the literature: Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) find employment fell more for young workers in higher-AI-exposure occupations, while Eckhardt and Goldschlag (2025) find the *opposite* — unemployment rose *less* in higher-exposure occupations. Iscenko and Millet (2026) muddy things further, finding job-posting declines in AI-exposed roles began in 2022, *before* ChatGPT's release, implicating macro conditions rather than AI causation. Kolko's three structural critiques of the field: (1) no consensus AI-exposure measure exists across studies, so "high exposure" means different things in different papers; (2) economic effects take years to show up while researchers are working with 1–2 years of noisy data; (3) research is overwhelmingly focused on labor demand while neglecting productivity, labor supply, and transition dynamics. He also flags a subtler risk — "narrator's bias," where AI researchers are disproportionately AI users themselves, potentially skewing interpretation. Genuinely valuable as an antidote to overconfident claims from either the doom or boom camp this cycle.

## Atlanta Fed CFO Survey: Productivity Gains Are Real and Revenue-Driven, Not Capital-Deepening — And Firms See Little Near-Term Employment Risk

`productivity` `labor` `empirical` `survey` `academic` · **Source:** [Atlanta Fed / NBER Working Paper 34984 — AI, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/working-papers/2026/03/25/04-artificial-intelligence-productivity-and-the-workforce-evidence-from-corporate-executives) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

A companion paper to the "Firm Data on AI" study covered last cycle, drawn from the same Atlanta Fed/Richmond Fed/Duke CFO Survey (603 CFO responses, fielded Nov–Dec 2025, ~750 executives total). Nearly 60% of firms invested in AI in 2025 (80% of large firms, ~50% of small firms). The productivity finding cuts against the pure "capex outrunning value" narrative: gains are positive, concentrated in high-skill services and finance, expected to strengthen in 2026, and — notably — driven by revenue-based total factor productivity (innovation/demand channels) rather than simple capital deepening, a more optimistic mechanism than the BLS capital-stock story flagged last cycle. On employment: "little evidence" firms have experienced or anticipate near-term AI-driven headcount declines, though larger firms expect workforce "shuffling" away from routine clerical work toward skilled technical roles — consistent with the seniority-bias pattern emerging across sources this cycle.

**More:** [NBER — Firm Data on AI (companion paper, w34836)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)

## PitchBook Fills the Valuation Gap: AI Startups Carry a ~4x Premium at Series D+, 84% at Series A

`funding` `valuation` `investment-research` `tech` · **Source:** [PitchBook — Q1 2026 US VC Valuations and Returns Report](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q1-2026-us-vc-valuations-and-returns-report) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

Fills the median-valuation gap flagged last cycle. AI companies command a structural premium over non-AI peers at every stage: Seed $18.7M vs. $18.0M (roughly flat), Series A $78.0M vs. $42.4M (84% premium), Series B $270.8M vs. $174.0M, Series C $606.5M vs. $507.3M, and Series D+ $4.7B vs. $1.3B median pre-money — roughly a 4x premium at the latest stage. Step-ups between rounds are also accelerating faster for AI companies (1.9x median vs. 1.6x for non-AI, as of March 31, 2026). The premium is widest at the *earliest* priced stage (Series A) relative to non-AI peers, which argues against a purely "mega-round distortion" reading — the market is pricing in an AI premium structurally, not just at the OpenAI/Anthropic tail.

## PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer: Top-Quintile AI-Exposed Firms Grew Productivity 163% Since 2018; AI-Skills Wage Premium Hits 62%

`productivity` `wages` `labor` `report` `survey` · **Source:** [PwC — 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

PwC's job-ad-based barometer (Tier 3 — consulting firm with an obvious commercial interest in the AI-advisory narrative, but the methodology is large-N job-postings analysis rather than self-reported sentiment) finds the top 20% most AI-exposed companies grew labor productivity 163% relative to 2018 — nearly 5x the average for all AI-exposed companies. AI-exposed sectors overall grew productivity 34% vs. 24% for the least AI-exposed. Jobs explicitly requiring AI skills (prompt engineering, ML) grew roughly 8x faster (69%) than the overall jobs market (9%), and the average wage premium for AI-skilled workers hit 62%, up from 57% a year ago. Read this alongside the PitchBook and Stanford Index data above: it's a third independent source this cycle showing gains concentrating sharply among already-advantaged firms and workers — strengthening "distribution matters more than magnitude" further, though treat the specific magnitudes with the usual vendor-study caution.

JPMorgan estimates global AI-related capex will reach $5.5T through 2030 and — unlike last cycle's Woo/Yardeni split, which stayed mostly at the level of opinion — backs its "not currently a bubble" call with real revenue growth figures: AWS AI-cloud revenue ~$150B annualized (+28% YoY), Google Cloud ~$80B (+63% YoY), Azure AI ~$37B run-rate (+123% YoY), all per Q1 2026 earnings. The bank's own conditional framing matters: it says the buildout is "profitable for now," and separately notes markets are only rewarding higher capex guidance when it's matched with higher revenue estimates — i.e., investor tolerance for pure "trust the buildout" spending (flagged as thinning last cycle) is being enforced by the market itself, not just commentators. Sequoia's David Cahn separately reiterates the ~$600B annual revenue gap needed to justify total AI infrastructure spend — the same order of magnitude flagged last cycle, still unresolved.

## Acemoglu's Research Agenda Broadens: Three New NBER Papers on Knowledge, Aggregation, and Political Economy

`academic` `nber` `paper` `contrarian` · **Source:** [NBER — AI, Human Cognition and Knowledge Collapse (w34910)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34910) · *Found: 2026-07-13*

Beyond the Autor collaboration above, Acemoglu has three additional new NBER working papers this cycle, signaling his research agenda is expanding beyond pure macro/productivity estimation: "AI, Human Cognition and Knowledge Collapse" (with Dingwen Kong and Asuman Ozdaglar, w34910), "How AI Aggregation Affects Knowledge" (with Tianyi Lin, Ozdaglar, and James Siderius, w35036), and "Automation and Repression" (with A. Arda Gitmez and Mehdi Shadmehr, w35336) — the latter a political-economy paper on automation's use in authoritarian contexts, a notable departure from his usual labor/growth focus. None of these directly move the productivity-magnitude debate, but they matter as a signal: the most-cited AI skeptic economist is diversifying into epistemics (knowledge collapse from AI-mediated information) and political economy rather than doubling down solely on TFP estimation — worth tracking for whether any of these become as influential as "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI."
