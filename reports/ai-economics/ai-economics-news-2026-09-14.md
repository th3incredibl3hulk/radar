# AI Economics News Report — 2026-09-14

## Executive Summary

The systemic-risk chorus got its most quantified entry yet. The BIS's September Quarterly Review (published today, 2026-09-14) put hard numbers on the debt-and-opacity concern it's been circling since its June Annual Report: aggregate tech-firm borrowing has gone from $22B (22% of total private credit) in 2010 to over $1 trillion (44%) by 2025, with AI-specific private credit growing from ~$3B to ~$40B over the same span — much of it off-balance-sheet, circular, and, per BIS official Frank Smets, "quite opaque." This isn't a new institution joining the warning list (still Fed, BoE, IMF, ECB, BIS as repeat voices) — it's the same voices getting more specific about the mechanism, which is arguably more useful than another headline number.

On the productivity side, this cycle produced the most direct empirical test yet of whether firm-level AI gains are real: a new NBER paper (Babina, He & Jiang, w35684) finds firms increasing AI-skilled employment from 2018-2024 saw roughly one additional percentage point of productivity growth per year — but explicitly *not* in the prior decade, and the mechanism is "organizational capital" (firm-specific process knowledge), not raw automation. That's a genuinely causal-adjacent, firm-level positive result sitting right next to Goldman's own finding of "no meaningful relationship between AI and productivity at the economy-wide level" — the firm/economy aggregation gap this report has tracked for two months just got another concrete data point on the firm side.

The most consequential single release, though, is Anthropic's new economic-scenario tool, built on a genuine technical paper (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter & McCrory, "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI") rather than marketing collateral. It models three 2030 outcomes — GDP +1.6%/+8.3%/+32.4% above no-AI baseline — and, notably, was reviewed pre-publication by Acemoglu, Autor, and David Romer, none of whom were asked to endorse it. Acemoglu's own reaction (AI keeps improving fast, but diffuses more slowly than the scenarios suggest) is the cleanest real-time read yet on where Tier-1 skepticism currently sits relative to a frontier lab's own modeling. Labor markets stayed noisy at the margins: Oracle appears to be planning a second, larger 2026 layoff round explicitly to fund its AI capex gap (after already cutting 21,000 in FY2026 while posting -$23.7B free cash flow), even as Uber's ~3,300-person cut in the same week was explicitly *not* framed as AI-driven — a reminder that "tech layoffs" and "AI layoffs" remain overlapping but distinct categories that get conflated in the tracker data.

## BIS Quarterly Review Sharpens the AI-Debt Warning With Hard Numbers

`capex` `investment-research` `valuation` `global` `measured` · **Source:** [BIS Quarterly Review, September 2026 via Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2026/09/14/884937.htm) · *Found: 2026-09-14*

The BIS's latest Quarterly Review (published today) quantifies what its June Annual Report flagged qualitatively: tech-firm borrowing has risen from $22B (22% of total private credit) in 2010 to over $1 trillion (44%) by 2025, against roughly $2.5 trillion in total private-credit loans outstanding; AI-specific private credit grew from ~$3B to ~$40B over the same period. BIS official Frank Smets was explicit about the mechanism: "many of these financing deals are quite opaque... often off balance sheet... [with] sort of circularity in them." The BIS also links rising AI debt issuance to upward pressure on government bond yields. This is an escalation in specificity, not a new institution — Fed, BoE, IMF, and ECB have all separately warned on AI-linked correction risk since July — but it's the sharpest quantification of the "opacity" thread this report has tracked since Chicago Booth's coverage of the Apollo/Blackstone SPV in August.

## Anthropic Publishes Its Own AI-Economics Model — Reviewed, Not Endorsed, by Acemoglu and Autor

`gdp` `growth` `labor` `forecast` `academic` `us` `measured` · **Source:** [Anthropic, "Scenarios for our Economic Future"](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios) · *Found: 2026-09-14*

Anthropic released an interactive scenario explorer built on a genuine technical paper — "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" by Anton Korinek (lead), Charles I. Jones, Szymon Sacher, Tess Cotter, and Peter McCrory. The model treats jobs as bundles of tasks AI can leave alone, augment, automate, or use to spawn new tasks, and runs three scenarios to 2030: "modest" (+1.6% GDP vs. no-AI baseline), "substantial" (AI handles ~50% of knowledge work autonomously, GDP +8.3% to $36.3T, knowledge-worker wages stagnate while other workers gain), and "extreme" (+32.4% GDP, capital's share of income rising to 54.8%, knowledge-worker wages falling ~10%). Notably, Acemoglu, Autor, and David Romer reviewed an early draft — Anthropic states they were not asked to endorse the conclusions. Acemoglu's own comment (to NPR): he expects capability to keep improving fast but diffusion to be slower than the scenarios imply, consistent with his standing published position. Treat this as a serious, transparent modeling exercise from a lab with an obvious commercial stake in AI mattering economically — useful for the scenario logic, not as an independent forecast.

## New NBER Paper Finds Firm-Level AI Productivity Gains Are Real — But Only Since 2018, and Only Via "Organizational Capital"

`productivity` `academic` `nber` `empirical` `us` `measured` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 35684 — Babina, He & Jiang](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35684) · *Found: 2026-09-14*

"Canaries in the Gold Mine: Early Productivity Gains from Artificial Intelligence Creating Organization Capital" builds a firm-level AI-investment measure from AI-skilled employment (spanning classic ML through generative/agentic AI) and finds a one-standard-deviation increase in a firm's AI-worker growth predicts 6.2pp greater six-year sales-per-worker growth and 7.5pp greater six-year TFP growth — roughly a full additional percentage point of productivity growth per year. Critically, the same relationship does *not* hold over the prior decade, and the gains trace specifically to AI-skilled jobs that build durable, firm-specific "organizational capital" (AI jobs are 58% likely to build such capital vs. 31% for non-AI jobs) — not to automation-driven headcount reduction. Robust to controls for acquisitions, offshoring, pre-existing trends, and industry/pandemic shocks, though the underlying identification is correlational rather than a clean natural experiment. This is a genuinely new empirical anchor for the firm-level side of the aggregation puzzle — it sits directly next to Goldman's now-familiar finding of no economy-wide AI-productivity relationship, sharpening rather than resolving that gap. Surfaced via ITIF's "Fact of the Week" (2026-09-08).

## Catch-Up: Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine" Update Shows the Young-Worker AI Employment Gap Widening to 19%

`labor` `academic` `displacement` `empirical` `us` `measured` · **Source:** [Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (revised)](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/) · *Found: 2026-09-14*

Flagging this now because it fell into a gap between our last two report windows (published 2026-08-12) and was never captured. Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen's revised paper, using ADP payroll data through June 2026, finds employment for workers aged 22-25 in highly AI-exposed occupations now runs 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers — up from 15% when they first measured this in July 2025 and widening steadily since. Experienced workers in the same occupations show no comparable gap; the effect runs through reduced hiring of young workers, not increased separations, and is concentrated in occupations where AI usage substitutes for rather than complements human tasks. The authors are explicit that these are "descriptive patterns, not causal estimates" — the data can't cleanly separate AI's effect from other forces (rate environment, hiring caution generally). This is the single most-cited empirical result in the entry-level-displacement literature and belongs in this report's baseline; it should have appeared in the 2026-08-17 or 2026-08-24 cycle and didn't — a genuine coverage miss, now corrected.

## Contrarian Take

### Anthropic's Economic Scenario Explorer
Anthropic's model is genuinely rigorous — task-based, three explicit scenarios, transparent about limitations — but it's still a frontier lab publishing a tool whose more dramatic scenarios (GDP +32.4%, capital share to 54.8%) implicitly argue that the thing Anthropic sells is worth an enormous amount, and it launches during the same week the BIS is warning that circular AI financing is already showing "signs of vulnerability." The strongest counter-evidence isn't rhetorical, it's Acemoglu's own reaction to reviewing the draft: he expects capability gains to keep coming but diffusion to run slower than Anthropic's "substantial" and "extreme" scenarios assume — a Tier 1 economist with a published, decade-long track record of arguing exactly this (his ~0.7% cumulative TFP estimate is an order of magnitude below Anthropic's "substantial" case) declining to co-sign the framework he was shown. Goldman's parallel finding of "no meaningful relationship between AI and productivity at the economy-wide level" is the same story from a different, commercially disinterested source: impressive modeling of what *could* happen is not evidence of what *is* happening, and the gap between the two is currently the whole debate.

### NBER's "Organizational Capital" Productivity Finding
Babina, He & Jiang's result is a genuine empirical contribution, but it's easy to over-read as resolving the productivity paradox in the optimists' favor — it doesn't. The paper measures firms that chose to grow AI-skilled employment, which is exactly the kind of firm most likely to already have strong management and absorptive capacity (the paper's own "organizational capital" mechanism is arguably a proxy for pre-existing firm quality, not proof AI caused the gain). Set against BLS's total factor productivity deceleration from 1.5% (2024) to 0.8% (2025) even as AI-linked software investment grew 11.1%/year, and Goldman's economy-wide null finding, the more defensible read is exactly what this report has argued for two months: real, positive, but *concentrated* firm-level gains that are not yet aggregating — this cycle adds a data point to the "concentrating" side of the distribution debate, not a resolution of the magnitude debate.
