# AI Economics News Report — 2026-07-27

## Executive Summary

This week the capex-vs-value tension that's been building in the data for two months finally hit the tape. Alphabet's Q2 2026 print (2026-07-22) beat on revenue and grew Google Cloud 82% YoY — and the stock still fell over 7% and dragged the "Magnificent Seven" index down 4.8% in a single session, its worst day since April 2025, because Alphabet raised full-year capex guidance to $195–205B and posted negative free cash flow for the first time since its 2004 IPO. The market's message, in one analyst's words: "It used to be the more [capex] the better, but now it is the less the better." Microsoft and Meta report Tuesday (2026-07-29); this cycle's report is a preview of what could be a rougher week ahead. Separately, SpaceX — barely two months off its IPO — has lost roughly $1 trillion in peak market value, with Morgan Stanley calculating that a further slide to $100/share would imply the market assigns *zero* value to its AI division (Q1 2026: $818M revenue against a $2.47B operating loss). Read together, these are the clearest market-level data points yet for the "AI capex is ahead of AI value" thesis that BIS, Bain, and Goldman's own J-curve analysis have been building empirically over the past month.

On labor and wages, this cycle filled several long-standing gaps in this report's coverage. A targeted named-author search surfaced David Autor's "Resolving the Automation Paradox" (with B.N. Kausik) — a genuinely counterintuitive Tier 1 theoretical result arguing that in economies where labor share already exceeds its wage-maximizing level (which the authors estimate is true in the US and eleven other industrialized countries), *further* automation should raise wages, not depress them, by shifting the capital-labor ratio toward a more wage-favorable point. That's a sharp complication for the simple "automation shrinks the wage pie" intuition that undergirds a lot of the displacement-anxiety literature. On the empirical side, the IMF's own staff research (Tier 1, filling this report's geographic/wage gap simultaneously) finds AI-skill demand is real and wage-positive but polarizing — benefiting high-skill and, via demand spillovers, low-skill service workers, while hollowing the middle. PwC's job-ad-based Barometer and Indeed's postings data both corroborate a "two-track" labor market: AI-skill wage premiums keep widening (62%, up from 57% YoY) even as enterprise-side ROI stays stubbornly elusive — PwC's CEO survey finds 56% of firms report zero net financial benefit from AI to date, and only 12% report gains on both cost and revenue simultaneously. The "distribution matters more than magnitude" thread just got its most direct wage-side confirmation yet.

## Autor's "Resolving the Automation Paradox": Since US Labor Share Is Already Too High to Maximize Wages, More Automation Should Raise Pay

`labor` `wages` `academic` `contrarian` `measured` · **Source:** [SSRN — Autor & Kausik, "Resolving the Automation Paradox"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6053214) · *Found: 2026-07-27 (paper dated December 2025; surfaced this cycle via targeted named-author search, not previously covered)*

David Autor (MIT, Tier 1) and B.N. Kausik prove a genuinely counterintuitive theoretical result: in a competitive economy with constant returns to scale, the labor share that *maximizes* wages depends only on the capital-to-labor ratio — implying a non-monotonic relationship between labor share and wages. When labor share exceeds this wage-maximizing threshold (as the authors estimate it does in the US and eleven other industrialized economies they examine), further automation that *reduces* labor's output share can nonetheless *raise* wages, because it shifts the capital-labor ratio toward a more productive equilibrium. This directly complicates the intuitive "automation shrinks the wage pie" framing that much of the displacement-anxiety literature assumes. It's a theoretical/structural result, not a causal empirical estimate — worth flagging that distinction — but it's a genuinely novel contrarian angle from the field's most prominent labor economist not yet reflected in this report's Great Debate tracker on wages. Fills a gap flagged in this report's own memory: Autor had not produced a distinct 2026 finding beyond the co-authored "Building Pro-Worker AI" framework (covered 2026-07-13).

**More:** [MIT Stone Center on Inequality and Shaping the Future of Work](https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/resolving-the-automation-paradox-falling-labor-share-rising-wages/) · [LSE PoID abstract](https://poid.lse.ac.uk/PUBLICATIONS/abstract.asp?index=12005)

## IMF Staff Research: AI Skills Raise Wages but Deepen Labor-Market Polarization, Squeezing the Middle

`labor` `wages` `inequality` `academic` `global` `measured` · **Source:** [IMF — Bridging Skill Gaps for the Future: New Jobs Creation in the AI Age (SDN/2026/001)](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/staff-discussion-notes/issues/2026/01/09/bridging-skill-gaps-for-the-future-new-jobs-creation-in-the-ai-age-572136) · *Found: 2026-07-27 (published January 2026; surfaced this cycle, not previously covered)*

IMF staff (Jaumotte, Kim, Koll, Li, Li, Melina, Song, Mendes Tavares — Tier 1) find that roughly one in ten job vacancies in advanced economies now demands at least one "new skill," concentrated in IT and AI and appearing first in the United States before diffusing to emerging economies (about half the incidence). Vacancies demanding AI skills post meaningfully higher wages — but the diffusion of those skills is linked to *lower* employment in occupations that are highly AI-exposed and have low complementarity with AI, a combination the authors flag as a particular challenge for youth labor-market entry. The net distributional effect: new skills raise average wages and employment but deepen polarization, benefiting high-skill workers directly and low-skill service workers indirectly (via consumption spillovers), while potentially hollowing out the middle. This is the first Tier 1, globally-scoped (not US-only) empirical wage/skills paper this report has surfaced, and it corroborates rather than complicates the "distribution matters more than magnitude" thesis this report has been tracking since early July.

**More:** [IMF F&D — The Race for AI-Ready Workers](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/03/picture-this-the-race-for-ai-ready-workers) · [Policy Edge summary](https://www.policyedge.in/p/imf-bridging-skill-gaps-and-new-jobs)

## PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer: AI-Skills Wage Premium Hits 62%, Labor Market Splits Into "Professionalized" vs. "Democratized" Tracks

`labor` `wages` `survey` `report` `global` · **Source:** [PwC — 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/artificial-intelligence/job-barometer/2026/2026-global-ai-jobs-barometer-global-findings.pdf) · *Found: 2026-07-27 (published 2026-06-15; surfaced this cycle, fills wage/skills-premium gap flagged in this report's memory)*

PwC's job-ad-based Barometer (Tier 3 — consulting-firm research; PwC sells AI advisory, a real conflict of interest, though the underlying dataset of 1B+ job ads across 27 countries is genuinely large-N) finds the average wage premium for AI skills rose to 62%, up from 57% a year prior — as high as 118% in consumer markets, as low as 16% in government/public-sector roles. The report's more structurally interesting finding: labor markets are splitting into "professionalised" roles (AI automates routine tasks, elevating human judgment — e.g., radiologists, recruiters), which are growing at roughly twice the rate and with 42% faster salary growth than "democratised" roles (AI lowers the skill bar for entry — e.g., IT service managers, medical secretaries). AI-skill job postings are growing ~8x faster than the overall job market. Read alongside the IMF paper above, this is now three independent sources (IMF, PwC, and this report's own Indeed/Anthropic tracking since June) converging on the same structural claim: AI is not eliminating jobs in aggregate, but it is sorting workers into faster-growing/higher-paid and slower-growing/lower-paid tracks based on complementarity, not just exposure.

## Indeed Hiring Lab: AI-Mentioning Job Postings Up 130%+; AI Job Titles Have Tripled Since 2022 and Now Mostly Live Outside Tech

`labor` `data` `empirical` `us` · **Source:** [Indeed Hiring Lab — AI and Job Postings: From Destruction to Creation?](https://www.hiringlab.org/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) · *Found: 2026-07-27 (fills the AI-job-postings gap flagged in this report's memory since 2026-07-11)*

Job postings mentioning AI or AI-related terms are up more than 130% on Indeed, even as broader hiring stays weak. More striking: the number of distinct US job titles referencing AI has more than tripled since 2022 (264 → 822 titles by Q1 2026), and nearly two-thirds (63%) of those titles now sit outside traditional tech occupations — healthcare, education, marketing, logistics, and management. Indeed's follow-up "US Labor Market Snapshot — June 2026" (2026-07-23, squarely in this reporting window) confirms the pattern is still accelerating, with data-center-adjacent trades (electricians, installation/maintenance technicians) also seeing AI-infrastructure-driven demand growth. This is genuinely useful because it's postings data, not survey sentiment — and it directly complicates a pure "AI destroys jobs" narrative by showing the technology generating a wide, non-tech-concentrated set of new job titles even as it automates specific tasks within existing roles.

## PwC's 2026 Global CEO Survey: 56% of CEOs Report Zero Net Financial Benefit From AI; Only 12% See Gains on Both Cost and Revenue

`productivity` `survey` `report` `contrarian` `earnings` · **Source:** [PwC — 29th Global CEO Survey](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/ceo-survey/2026/pwc-ceo-survey-2026.pdf) · *Found: 2026-07-27*

PwC surveyed 4,454 CEOs across 95 countries and found 56% report no significant financial benefit from AI to date, while only 12% report gains on *both* cost reduction and revenue growth (33% report gains on either). The gap tracks deployment maturity: CEOs reporting dual gains are 2-3x more likely to have embedded AI extensively across products, demand generation, and strategic decision-making, and 3x more likely to have "AI foundations" (responsible-AI frameworks, integration-ready tech environments) in place. Standard caveat applies — self-reported CEO sentiment, and PwC sells AI-adoption advisory services, a direct conflict of interest on the "adoption is worth it" framing — but this is now the third independent enterprise-survey data point in six weeks (after Bain's 951-firm ROI-gap survey on 2026-07-20 and McKinsey's 39%-EBIT-impact figure from earlier in July) converging on the same "deployment is nearly universal, realized value capture is not" finding. Treat the direction as robust across sources with different commercial incentives; treat the exact percentages with appropriate skepticism given self-report bias in all three.
