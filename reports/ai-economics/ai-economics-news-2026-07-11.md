# AI Economics News Report — 2026-07-11

## Executive Summary

This is the inaugural report in this series, covering roughly the last two weeks (2026-06-27 to 2026-07-11). The dominant theme is a widening split between the *capex/funding* story and the *productivity/labor* story. Money continues to pour in at absurd scale — $355.9B (86% of all US venture dollars) went to AI companies in H1 2026, hyperscalers guided to $725B combined 2026 capex (+77% YoY), and Anthropic alone raised $65B in a single quarter. Meanwhile the hard productivity data is not keeping pace: BLS total factor productivity decelerated from 1.5% to 0.8% in 2025 even as software capex hit record growth, and McKinsey's own survey shows adoption is nearly universal (88%) but scaled value capture is not (only 39% report enterprise-level EBIT impact). That gap — real spend, unconfirmed macro return — is now the central tension in the debate.

On labor, Goldman Sachs raised its 10-year AI displacement estimate from 6–7% to over 9% of the US workforce (~15M workers) but explicitly rejects "job apocalypse" framing, arguing net effects are a modest drag rather than a collapse. Acemoglu's numbers, refreshed in a widely covered mid-2026 round of interviews, remain the most bearish of any credible economist: ~0.07pp/year TFP gain, 0.7% cumulative over a decade — an order of magnitude below Wall Street's 1–2%/year consensus. The youth-unemployment story that's been circulating as "AI is eating entry-level jobs" is getting contested by the Fed's own researchers, who attribute most of the effect to weak overall hiring rather than AI substitution — though the Atlanta Fed is separately worried about a slower-burn effect: automation eroding the "learning by doing" pipeline that trains future senior workers.

The most quotable moment of the period was Palantir CEO Alex Karp's on-air rant that frontier labs' token-based pricing has been "completely, irresponsibly oversold" to enterprises getting no measurable ROI — a Tier-3 claim with an obvious conflict of interest (Palantir competes via open-weight/on-prem deployments with Nvidia), but one that rhymes with the Fed's own financial-stability concerns: 50% of surveyed market contacts now flag AI as a systemic risk, up from 30% six months earlier, with AI capex increasingly funded by debt.

## Acemoglu's Productivity Estimate Holds at the Bearish Pole

`productivity` `growth` `academic` `contrarian` `skeptic` · **Source:** [MIT Technology Review — Three things in AI to watch, according to a Nobel-winning economist](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/11/1137090/three-things-in-ai-to-watch-according-to-a-nobel-winning-economist/) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

Daron Acemoglu (MIT, Nobel laureate) continues to hold the most bearish credible position on AI's macro impact: roughly 0.07 percentage points of annual TFP growth, cumulating to ~0.7% over a decade — consistent with his earlier "Simple Macroeconomics of AI" framework. He estimates only ~5% of tasks are profitably automatable near-term, implying a 1–1.5% GDP boost at most. This is roughly an order of magnitude below Goldman's and a16z's optimist-camp estimates of 1–2%/year. Acemoglu separately flags three things he's watching for 2026: agentic AI deployments, the hiring race for in-house AI-lab economists (OpenAI's Ronnie Chatterji and Jason Furman, Anthropic's 10-member advisory council, DeepMind's new "director of AGI economics"), and the still-missing layer of usable consumer apps.

## Goldman Raises Displacement Estimate to 15M Workers, Still Rejects "Apocalypse" Framing

`labor` `displacement` `augmentation` `investment-research` `forecast` · **Source:** [Goldman Sachs Research — An AI Job Apocalypse?](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-research/an-ai-job-apocalypse/report.pdf) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

Goldman raised its 10-year US displacement estimate from 6–7% to over 9% of the workforce (~15M workers), and now says AI is shaving 10,000–15,000 jobs off monthly employment growth in the most-exposed sectors (tech, management consulting, graphic design). Despite the headline, the report's conclusion pushes back on doom framing: augmentation-driven job creation partially offsets substitution, producing a "small net drag" rather than mass unemployment. Notably, Goldman says there's "little evidence so far" that AI has meaningfully hurt recent college graduates' employment prospects — a data point that cuts against the popular "AI is killing entry-level jobs" narrative (see below).

## Fed Financial Stability Report: AI Now the #1 Cited Systemic Risk, Increasingly Debt-Funded

`gdp` `growth` `capex` `regulation` `academic` · **Source:** [Federal Reserve — Financial Stability Report, Spring 2026](https://cryptobriefing.com/fed-spring-2026-geopolitical-ai-risks/) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

50% of market contacts surveyed for the Fed's Spring 2026 Financial Stability Report cited AI as a possible shock to the system, up from 30% in Fall 2025 and just 9% a year prior — the fastest-rising risk category in the survey. The Fed flags that AI capex is "increasingly funded by debt, creating leverage in the system," and separately, the Chicago Fed notes banks' committed exposure to AI-adjacent industries has risen from ~9% of C&I commitments in 2015 to 13% in late 2025, averaging ~25% of Tier 1 capital at large banks. The IMF's April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report independently flagged stretched AI-related valuations as a "material downside risk," warning an abrupt repricing could spill over into broader credit markets. This is a genuine escalation from central-bank institutions — not commentary, but survey and exposure data.

## BLS: Productivity Growth Decelerating Even as AI-Linked Software Capex Hits Record Pace

`productivity` `gdp` `academic` `empirical` `data` · **Source:** [BLS — Productivity and Costs, First Quarter 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

Nonfarm business labor productivity rose just 0.3% quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026 (2.9% year-over-year, itself a slowdown). More striking: total factor productivity — the efficiency-isolated measure — decelerated from 1.5% to 0.8% across 2025, even as software investment (BLS's proxy for AI capital deployment) grew 11.1% annually 2019–2024, the fastest of any asset category by a wide margin. Read plainly: the capex is showing up in the capital stock, not yet in measured efficiency. This is the sharpest hard-data evidence yet for "Productivity Paradox 2.0" — AI spending is visible everywhere except, so far, in aggregate productivity statistics.

## McKinsey: Adoption Is Nearly Universal, Scaling Is Not — Only 39% Report Enterprise EBIT Impact

`productivity` `survey` `report` `services` `measured` · **Source:** [McKinsey — State of AI Trust in 2026: Shifting to the Agentic Era](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/tech-forward/state-of-ai-trust-in-2026-shifting-to-the-agentic-era) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

88% of surveyed organizations regularly use AI in at least one function (72% use gen AI regularly, up from 33% in 2024), and 72% have at least one AI workload in production, up from just 20% in 2020. But the scaling gap is stark: only 23% report scaling an agentic AI system anywhere in the enterprise, no single business function exceeds 10% agent-scaling penetration, and just 39% report EBIT impact at the enterprise level despite widespread use-case-level deployment. This is the clearest survey-based confirmation of the "distribution matters more than magnitude" thesis — adoption ≠ value capture, and the gap between the two is where most of the corporate AI story currently sits.

This is capital markets making a two-company bet inside a one-sector bet — a fragility worth flagging regardless of one's productivity-debate priors.

## Fed Researchers Split on Youth Unemployment: Weak Hiring, Not AI, Is the Bigger Driver — But a Slower "Learning by Doing" Risk Looms

`labor` `displacement` `academic` `wages` `contrarian` · **Source:** [St. Louis Fed — How Shifts in Labor Supply and Demand Shape Outcomes for Young Workers](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2026/jun/how-shifts-labor-supply-demand-shape-outcomes-young-workers) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

The popular narrative that AI is gutting entry-level hiring is getting a more careful look from the Fed system itself. In occupations most exposed to AI, young-worker employment fell 6% between late 2022 and September 2025 while older-worker employment in the same roles rose 6–9% — but separate NY Fed-adjacent research attributes the bulk of rising 18–24 unemployment (2.9pp between April 2023–December 2025) to a general shortage of job openings, with the AI-related occupational shift accounting for only ~1.1pp of that increase. Meanwhile the Atlanta Fed is reviving Arrow's 1962 "learning by doing" theory to warn of a subtler, slower risk: firms automating away the junior tasks that train future senior talent, potentially eviscerating the skill pipeline years out. They float a tax on automation-derived profits paired with subsidies for firms that preserve entry-level task exposure.

## NBER: 69% of Firms Actively Use AI, but Execs Average Only 1.5 Hours/Week With It

`productivity` `empirical` `nber` `academic` `paper` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 34836 — Firm Data on AI](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836) · *Found: 2026-07-11*

Barrero, Yotzov et al.'s new NBER paper on firm-level AI usage finds 69% of firms actively use AI, with adoption skewed toward younger and more-productive firms — consistent with the "distribution matters more than magnitude" thesis (adoption compounds existing firm advantages rather than leveling the field). More strikingly: even at firms with AI in active use, executives average just 1.5 hours per week actually using it, despite two-thirds of executives being regular users. Real causal identification is limited here (this looks like a cross-sectional survey, not an RCT or natural experiment) — treat the adoption-productivity correlation with the usual caution.

Economist David Woo (July 4) predicts an AI bubble burst in H2 2026, citing elevated real yields sustained by the AI investment boom. No resolution here, but the fact that credible voices are now split roughly down the middle (rather than uniformly bullish, as in 2023–2024) is itself the story.
