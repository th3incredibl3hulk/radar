# AI Economics News Report — 2026-08-03

## Executive Summary

Last week's Alphabet-triggered "market revolt" on AI capex turned out to be the opening act, not the whole show. Five hyperscalers reported Q2 2026 between July 22 and July 30, and the market drew a sharp, consistent line: capex tied to metered, visibly-demanded cloud consumption gets rewarded; capex framed as "investment-first" gets punished, regardless of headline revenue strength. Microsoft (+8%, Azure +43%) and Amazon (+8-10%, AWS +37% — fastest in 18 quarters) rallied despite raising or maintaining aggressive spending plans, because analysts could trace the capex directly to backlog and utilization. Meta (-9.6% to -10%, despite 28% revenue growth) and Apple (-4% to -8%) both fell; Alphabet had already fallen 7% the week prior. Combined 2026 hyperscaler capex guidance is now $675-700B+ (Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet alone at $500-525B, plus Amazon's ~$173B trailing-twelve-month actual) — and the market's message, in one analyst's framing, is that it's now "pricing revenue legibility, not spend size." This is a genuine sharpening of the capex-vs-value debate, not just a repeat: the question isn't "is there too much AI capex" anymore, it's "which capex can you underwrite."

On the research side, this cycle filled two long-standing gaps and added a new theoretical fault line. Anton Korinek (Tier 1, UVA/Brookings) and Patrick McKelvey's "Measuring the AI Economy" — simultaneously published via CEPR, PIIE, and the Bank of Canada — estimates quality-adjusted AI output grew over 2,000%/year in 2024-2025, compounding compute-capacity growth (200%+/year) and nominal spending growth (140%+/year), and argues official GDP statistics are structurally failing to capture this activity. That's a rigorous, measurement-focused complement to the BLS TFP-deceleration puzzle this report has tracked since July — same paradox, sharper diagnosis of why it might be a statistical artifact rather than a real absence of value. Separately, the OECD's Employment Outlook 2026 (released 2026-07-07, filling this report's persistent non-US gap) corroborates the "no widespread displacement, but geography is splitting hard" finding already established by Fed and Stanford data — job vacancies in AI-exposed industries are *rising* faster than elsewhere across most OECD economies, even as regional disparities in wages and employment widen. And Daron Acemoglu (Tier 1, MIT) opened a new, more unsettling front: with Dingwen Kong and Asuman Ozdaglar, he models a "knowledge collapse" steady state where sufficiently accurate agentic AI recommendations crowd out the human learning that produces society's shared knowledge base — a theoretical result already drawing direct academic pushback, which is itself useful signal about how contested even Tier-1 AI-economics theory currently is.

Five hyperscalers reported Q2 2026 results between July 22 and July 30 — Alphabet (07-22, covered last cycle), Microsoft and Meta (07-29), Amazon and Apple (07-30) — and the market's reaction was not uniformly bearish, as last cycle's Alphabet sell-off might have suggested, but sharply bifurcated. Microsoft rose ~8% (Azure +43% YoY, FY27 capex guided to $255-260B on the back of an 84% surge in remaining performance obligations to $678B) and Amazon rose 8-10% (revenue $200.6B, +20% YoY, its first $200B quarter; AWS +37% YoY, the fastest growth in 18 quarters; AI/Chips run-rates each above $25B; 2026 capex guidance lifted from $200B to $220B). Meta fell 9.6-10% despite revenue of $60.8B (+28% YoY) — free cash flow collapsed ~91% YoY to $784M as $31.1B in quarterly capex consumed nearly all operating cash flow, and full-year capex guidance rose to $130-145B. Apple fell 4-8% on services misses and supply constraints despite record $109.4B revenue. Combined 2026 capex guidance across Microsoft, Meta, and Alphabet alone now sits at $500-525B; adding Amazon's ~$173B trailing-twelve-month actual spend puts the hyperscaler total north of $675-700B, above the $725B full-year figure this report has been tracking since July. The distinguishing factor investors are now pricing, per multiple analyst notes: whether capex maps to a *metered, visibly-growing* revenue line (Azure, AWS) or is described as *investment-first* with revenue proof deferred (Meta's ad-efficiency gains, Alphabet's cloud backlog). This is a meaningful sharpening of the "AI capex is ahead of AI value" thesis this report flagged as its top story last cycle: the market isn't broadly repricing all AI capex down, it's differentiating — which is arguably a more sophisticated (and more durable) form of skepticism than an undifferentiated sell-off.

## Korinek & McKelvey: Quality-Adjusted AI Output Grew Over 2,000%/Year — And Official GDP Statistics Are Missing It

`productivity` `gdp` `academic` `nber` `measured` · **Source:** [PIIE / CEPR / Bank of Canada — Korinek & McKelvey, "Measuring the AI Economy"](https://www.piie.com/publications/working-papers/2026/measuring-ai-economy) · *Found: 2026-08-02 (simultaneously released as PIIE WP26-9, CEPR DP21571, and Bank of Canada Staff Working Paper 2026-20; not previously covered)*

Anton Korinek (UVA/Brookings, Tier 1) and Patrick McKelvey construct a macroeconomic estimate of total US AI production — combining inference and R&D/training activity, quality-adjusted for the pace of algorithmic progress and falling API prices at fixed performance — and find nominal AI compute spending grew over 140%/year in both 2024 and 2025, raw compute capacity grew over 200%/year, and *quality-adjusted* AI output grew over 2,000%/year. The paper's central argument is methodological and consequential: standard industry classifications and GDP accounting scatter AI activity across sectors (software, professional services, information) in ways that structurally undercount it, and the authors propose a nascent "AI GDP" framework to track it as a coherent economic aggregate. This is directly relevant to the productivity-paradox puzzle this report has tracked since its first cycle — BLS's nonfarm TFP deceleration (1.5%→0.8% across 2025) sits awkwardly next to firm-level RCT evidence of large AI productivity gains, and Korinek's measurement critique offers a genuine candidate explanation: not that the gains aren't real, but that current statistical infrastructure isn't built to see them. Worth flagging the distinction from a causal empirical estimate — this is a measurement/accounting exercise, not a productivity-impact study — but it's a rigorous, Tier 1 contribution to *why* the paradox might be partly artifactual.

## Acemoglu's "Knowledge Collapse" Model: Sufficiently Accurate Agentic AI Could Crowd Out Human Learning — And Other Economists Are Pushing Back

`labor` `academic` `nber` `contrarian` `measured` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 34910 — Acemoglu, Kong & Ozdaglar, "AI, Human Cognition and Knowledge Collapse"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34910) · *Found: 2026-08-01 (paper dated February 2026; surfaced this cycle via targeted named-author search, not previously covered)*

Daron Acemoglu (MIT, Tier 1), Dingwen Kong, and Asuman Ozdaglar build a dynamic model in which successful decisions require combining shared, community-level "general knowledge" with individual context-specific knowledge, and human learning effort jointly produces a private signal and a public signal that accumulates into the community's knowledge stock. Their core (and genuinely counterintuitive) result: when human effort is sufficiently elastic and agentic AI recommendations exceed an accuracy threshold, the economy can tip into a "knowledge collapse" steady state — general knowledge erodes to near-zero over time, even though each individual is receiving high-quality personalized advice, because AI's accuracy removes the incentive for people to do the effortful learning that used to replenish the shared knowledge base. Welfare is non-monotone in agentic accuracy, implying a policy-relevant interior optimum and motivating information-design regulation rather than simple accuracy-maximization. This is a distinct contrarian thread from this report's existing wage/displacement debates — it's about epistemic/cognitive infrastructure, not jobs or output — and it's already drawing direct academic pushback (a competing substack analysis titled "Acemoglu et al (2026) are wrong about AI & Human Cognition" disputes the model's assumptions), which is itself worth tracking: even Tier 1 theoretical work in this field is not converging quickly, and readers should treat this as a live, contested hypothesis rather than settled analysis.

## OECD Employment Outlook 2026: No Widespread AI Displacement, But Regional and Wage Disparities Widen Sharply

`labor` `wages` `global` `report` `measured` · **Source:** [OECD Employment Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2026_7e710f54-en.html) · *Found: 2026-08-01 (released 2026-07-07; fills this report's recurring non-US geographic gap)*

The OECD's flagship annual labor-market report (Tier 1 institutional, launched 2026-07-07) finds job vacancies in AI-exposed industries have risen *more* than in other sectors across most OECD economies in the year to April 2026 — reinforcing, at a genuinely multi-country scale, the "no widespread displacement yet" finding this report has already documented via Fed, Stanford, and Anthropic US-specific data. But the report's central framing this year is geographic: average OECD unemployment held roughly stable at 4.9% (May 2026), yet real wage growth across OECD economies slowed to 2.2% in Q1 2026 (from 2.7% a year earlier), and local labor markets are being reshaped unevenly by the combination of trade and AI/automation shocks — some regions losing manufacturing employment while others gain service-sector and non-routine work. A genuinely two-sided data point worth flagging for the augmentation camp: the OECD's own survey work finds 4 in 5 workers say AI improved their job performance and 3 in 5 say it increased their enjoyment of work — self-reported sentiment, not causal evidence, but a rare positive worker-experience data point in a report otherwise focused on disparity and risk.

## Fed's July 2026 Beige Book: AI Is Reallocating Tasks Across 11 of 12 Districts, Not (Yet) Cutting Headcount

`labor` `productivity` `data` `us` · **Source:** [Federal Reserve — Beige Book, July 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/beigebook202607.htm) · *Found: 2026-08-01 (released mid-July 2026; not previously covered)*

The Fed's periodic qualitative survey of regional business contacts (Tier 1) found 11 of 12 districts reporting slight-to-moderate economic growth for the period covering late May through early July 2026, with manufacturing and construction activity notably lifted by data-center-related demand. On labor specifically, the report's cross-district pattern is consistent with this cycle's other findings: employers "generally maintained head counts at current levels" while continuing to invest in productivity-enhancing AI tools and reallocating tasks within existing headcount — the San Francisco Fed's Twelfth District report made this explicit. This is a real-time, qualitative-but-broad corroboration of the "augmentation and task reallocation, not net headcount reduction, is the dominant near-term pattern" finding that OECD, Fed research notes, and Stanford data have each separately supported this cycle and last — now from the Fed's own business-contact survey network rather than a single study.

PitchBook's valuations data show the AI premium widening sharply with company stage as of Q1 2026: Series A AI companies carry an 84% median pre-money valuation premium over non-AI peers ($78.0M vs. $42.4M); by Series D+, AI startups' median pre-money valuation ($4.7B) is roughly 4x non-AI peers' ($1.3B). Treat the 21% figure as low-conviction market-implied sentiment (thin, speculative markets), not a rigorous forecast — but it's a useful complement to this report's existing Tier 1/2 bubble-risk sources (BIS, Fed Financial Stability Report, IMF GFSR), all of which flag AI valuation concentration as a risk without assigning it a specific probability.
