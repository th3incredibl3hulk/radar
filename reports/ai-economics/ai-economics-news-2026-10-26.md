# AI Economics News Report — 2026-10-26

## Executive Summary

The story this cycle is financing, not productivity. In the week of 2026-10-14/15, AI-linked credit cracked in public: CoreWeave's bonds fell for a second day with yields above 15%, the stock dropped about 15.5% in one session, and the FT reported that bonds tied to some data-center projects (Meta, Nvidia, Oracle-linked) traded below par. The Nasdaq fell 1.9% on 10-14 and the semiconductor index logged six straight losing sessions. This is the first time the IMF's October warning (covered 2026-10-19: AI correction as a headline downside risk) showed up as price action in credit rather than as a scenario in a box.

The Fed chair then addressed it from an unexpected angle. On 2026-10-16 in Bangkok, Kevin Warsh called AI spending a "shock" that central bankers "have to recognize": investment in chips and data centers hits demand before supply, which is inflationary, and the path to productivity gains will be "very bumpy." The Fed raised rates in September (its first hike since 2023) and September CPI ran 3.7%. **Correction to the 2026-10-19 report:** that report said it found no new Fed-level AI remarks. This speech, from three days earlier, was a miss.

Caveat on coverage: this was a thin window for Tier 1 academic output. ## Fed Chair Warsh: AI spending is a "shock" and the road to productivity will be "very bumpy"

`productivity` `capex` `measured` `us` · **Source:** [ABC News, 2026-10-16](https://abcnews.com/Business/fed-chair-kevin-warsh-warns-ai-shock-economy/story?id=137315708) · *Found: 2026-10-26*

**Tier 1 speaker, single secondary report.** At an IMF-organized conference in Bangkok, Warsh said "you've got to recognize this as a shock," because AI investment lands on demand before the supply it builds, which pushes prices up unless supply keeps pace. He stayed optimistic on eventual productivity and wealth gains but said it is unclear how the market will be structured and who profits. Context: the Fed hiked a quarter point in September, September CPI was 3.7% (a four-month high, driven largely by gasoline from the Middle East conflict), and CME FedWatch showed about 86% odds of a hold on 2026-10-28. This is the Overton-window point to watch: the Fed chair treating AI capex as an inflationary demand shock puts it on the central bank's problem list alongside oil, not only on the upside list. Warsh's AI task force (due to report to the FOMC by year-end) has still produced nothing public.

## How much of US growth is AI capex? Between a third and a half, depending on the definition

`gdp` `capex` `data` `us` · **Source:** [ING Think, James Knightley](https://think.ing.com/opinions/how-much-is-ai-contributing-to-us-economic-growth/) · *Found: 2026-10-26*

**Tier 2/3 (bank macro commentary; method partly visible).** NBC's 2026-10-15 line that "nearly half of all US economy growth this year is attributable to tech-related spending" is unsourced in the piece. ING's breakdown shows why the number swings: information-processing, software and data-center investment is about 50% of year-over-year growth in Q2 (four-quarter average about 47%); computing, peripherals and software only is about 44%; subtracting net imports of computers and semiconductors drops it to about 36%. WSJ coverage of ING puts the full-year share near one-third. A blog (AI2Work) claims 53% of Q2's 1.5% annualized growth, which I could not verify, so ignore it. Takeaway for a VP: quote "a third to a half," and note that the import adjustment is the honest one, since much of the hardware is built abroad.

## Contrarian Take

### AI-linked credit cracks: CoreWeave bonds above 15% yield, data-center bonds below par
The "canary" reading assumes one leveraged neocloud is representative of the whole buildout. The IMF's own modeling (Tier 1, covered 2026-10-19) puts the direct effect of an AI-investment unwind at up to 0.4% of global GDP, about 1.1% with wealth effects, and says even its worst AI case would not push global growth to recession levels. The demand side hasn't cracked either: TSMC and ASML both reported in their Q3 results that demand exceeds capacity into 2027-28 (Tier 3, company results; see the 2026-10-19 report). The better distinction is between cash-funded hyperscalers and debt-funded intermediaries: the one number that argues the other way is the FactSet 93% capex-to-operating-cash-flow ratio (Tier 2/3, tracked since 2026-09-21), which says the cash-funded group has little cushion left either. So the contrarian case is that this is a credit-quality sort among weak borrowers, not yet a demand signal. It's a case, not a proof; the Anthropic growth report, if confirmed with figures, would weaken it.
