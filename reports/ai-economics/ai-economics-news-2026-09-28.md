# AI Economics News Report — 2026-09-28

## Executive Summary

The capex story got bigger and the valuation story got shakier in the same week. Goldman Sachs' September 24-26 research round pushed hyperscaler capital-expenditure forecasts to $800B for 2026 (+94% YoY), $1.2T for 2027, and $1.4T for 2028 — a cumulative $7.6T in AI infrastructure spend through 2031 — while simultaneously flagging that AI-infrastructure stock valuations have already compressed from 32x to 22x forward earnings since April. That's the market pricing in more spend and less confidence, at the same time. Meanwhile the OECD's September interim outlook credited "continued strength in AI-related investment" as a reason it raised global growth forecasts to 2.9% (2026) and 3.0% (2027) — the first time this report has seen a top-tier multilateral institution attribute a headline GDP revision partly to AI capex, rather than just warning about the risk it poses.

On the academic side, a genuinely novel methodological paper (NBER w35793, Blumenfeld/Hazell/Lian/Schaab) tries to measure AI's macroeconomic effect using financial markets rather than surveys or productivity statistics — and gets a startling number: AI has raised the market's expected present value of software-engineering productivity by the equivalent of a permanent 32.6% productivity increase, translating to a 3.6-6.5% GDP effect that reportedly *doubled* between late 2025 and mid-2026. That estimate is an order of magnitude above anything BLS's actual productivity data or this report's other tracked sources have shown — a gap worth holding onto rather than resolving, since it hinges entirely on whether markets are pricing AI's future correctly, which is exactly what the same week's valuation-compression data casts doubt on. Rounding out the cycle: McKinsey's new global AI survey shows roughly one in five organizations has reached a "scaling phase" with AI agents, and Stanford's Digital Economy Lab's standing composite read across twelve indicators remains "no decisive evidence" of an AI-driven economic takeoff — the Productivity Paradox 2.0 thread stays open.

## Goldman Sachs: Hyperscaler Capex Forecast Rises to $7.6T Through 2031 — As AI Infrastructure Stock Valuations Compress 32x to 22x

`capex` `investment-research` `valuation` `earnings` `us` `measured` · **Source:** [Bloomberg, "Goldman Sees Hyperscaler AI Capex Rising 50% to $1.2 Trillion"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/goldman-sees-hyperscaler-ai-capex-rising-50-to-1-2-trillion) · *Found: 2026-09-28*

Goldman's September 24-26 research round (US equity outlook plus a separate AI infrastructure note) raised its hyperscaler capex forecast materially: the top five hyperscalers are now projected to spend ~$800B in 2026 (+94% YoY), $1.2T in 2027 (+~50%), and $1.4T in 2028, for a cumulative $7.6T in AI infrastructure investment (compute, data centers, power) through 2031. Goldman also flagged that capex *growth* will decelerate sharply — from nearly 100% in 2026 to 54% in 2027 and 12% in 2028 — and, more strikingly, that AI infrastructure stocks' median valuation has already fallen from 32x forward EPS in April 2026 to 22x in September, even as the spending forecasts kept rising. Goldman puts the breakeven bar at roughly $300B in annual AI revenue for hyperscalers to justify the spend, against announced backlogs already exceeding $1.5T. This is the sharpest capex-vs-confidence divergence this report has tracked: the dollar figures keep climbing while the multiple the market is willing to pay for that exposure keeps falling — a market, not sentiment-survey, signal that cuts against last cycle's BofA Fund Manager Survey finding of cooling bubble-tail-risk concern.

## NBER: A Market-Based Measure Says AI Has Already Delivered a 32.6% Productivity Boost to Software Engineering — Double Its Late-2025 Level

`productivity` `gdp` `growth` `academic` `nber` `empirical` `tech` `us` `optimist` · **Source:** [NBER Working Paper 35793 — Blumenfeld, Hazell, Lian & Schaab](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35793) · *Found: 2026-09-28*

"The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel" takes a genuinely different approach to a question this report tracks constantly: instead of surveys, productivity statistics, or task-exposure indices, the authors infer AI's economic effect from financial markets — estimating each firm's stock-return sensitivity to an AI-stock index, scaled by that firm's share of payroll in software engineering, to build a real-time, forward-looking measure of AI's expected productivity contribution. Their finding: from November 2022 to December 2025, AI raised the market's expected present value of software-engineering productivity by the equivalent of a permanent 32.6% productivity increase, implying a 3.6% GDP effect in their baseline (6.5% if software-engineering gains also lift R&D productivity). The authors report that by mid-2026, "amid rapid progress in coding agents," this effect had more than doubled relative to end-2025. Exact NBER issue date wasn't independently confirmed via metadata, but the paper number sequence and its mid-2026 coding-agent discussion place it after this report's prior citations. The headline number is an order of magnitude above BLS's actual measured productivity growth (+1.4% q/q, +2.2% y/y as of Q2 2026) — a gap the paper itself doesn't resolve, since it measures market *expectations* of future gains, not realized output. See Contrarian Take below.

**More:** [Author's working paper PDF](https://www.jhazell.co.uk/papers/AI_Software.pdf) · [Chen Lian's site](https://lianchen.github.io/)

## OECD Raises Global Growth Forecast, Explicitly Credits AI Investment

`gdp` `growth` `forecast` `global` `optimist` · **Source:** [OECD Economic Outlook, Interim Report, September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-outlook-interim-report-september-2026_f751d02b-en.html) · *Found: 2026-09-28*

The OECD's September interim outlook ("Weathering Successive Shocks," published ~2026-09-23) raised its global GDP growth forecast to 2.9% for 2026 (from 2.8% in June) and 3.0% for 2027 (from 2.9%), and named "continued strength in AI-related investment" — alongside released oil reserves, lower Chinese energy imports, and a shift toward alternative fuels — as a factor sustaining production, trade, and growth despite geopolitical energy shocks. This is the first time in this report's tracking window that a Tier-1 multilateral institution has attributed part of a headline growth-forecast *upgrade* to AI capex specifically, rather than treating AI investment purely as a systemic risk (the framing from the BIS, Fed, ECB, BoE, and IMF's prior warnings). It doesn't resolve the tension — the same investment the OECD credits for near-term growth is the investment the central-bank chorus warns could unwind disorderly — but it's a genuine institutional data point on the optimist side of the magnitude debate, worth weighing against the skeptic-leaning BLS TFP data this report has cited all year. The OECD also raised its 2026 global inflation forecast to 4.1% (from 4.0%), a reminder that "AI is helping growth" and "AI is disinflationary" (a claim in a separate Goldman note this week) aren't obviously reconciled with a rising OECD inflation print.

## McKinsey's 2026 Global AI Survey: One in Five Organizations Has Reached "Scaling Phase," 28% Spend Over 10% of IT Budget on AI

`labor` `survey` `investment-research` `enterprise` `augmentation` `global` · **Source:** [McKinsey, "The State of AI: Global Survey 2026"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) · *Found: 2026-09-28*

McKinsey's latest global AI survey (published 2026-09-22) reports that roughly two in ten respondent organizations have reached the "scaling phase" with AI agents and software-coding agents specifically, 28% of organizations now spend more than 10% of their total IT budget on AI, and 60% expect to increase AI investment over the next year. This is an executive-survey instrument — the same category this report has repeatedly flagged as running well ahead of the Census Bureau's randomly sampled BTOS figure (19.8% of all US firms using AI in production, per the last cycle's finding) — so treat the "one in five at scaling phase" figure as describing large, AI-forward firms rather than the economy-wide base rate. Still, the IT-budget-share figure (28% >10%) is a useful new granular data point on enterprise AI spending intensity that this report hasn't previously had at this resolution.

## Contrarian Take

### AI Has Already Delivered a Permanent 32.6% Productivity Boost to Software Engineering (NBER w35793)
The paper's headline claim rests entirely on the assumption that AI-stock returns are an unbiased, efficient-market forecast of future productivity gains — but that's precisely the assumption this report's other institutions spent the year contesting. Goldman's own valuation data from the *same week* shows AI-infrastructure stocks compressing from 32x to 22x forward EPS since April, meaning the market's own pricing of AI's future value is already retreating even as this paper's market-implied productivity estimate reportedly doubled into mid-2026 — a direct internal contradiction depending on which slice of "the market" you sample. More fundamentally, Chad Syverson's long-standing methodological critique of AI productivity claims (echoed in Acemoglu's May 2026 MIT Technology Review interview, where he specifically flagged the absence of "broadly usable consumer apps" as the missing signal of real economic impact) is that expectations-based and survey-based measures of AI's value systematically outrun realized, BLS-measured output — and BLS's own Q2 2026 productivity print (+1.4% q/q, +2.2% y/y) sits nowhere near a 32.6% software-engineering productivity gain. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab reaches the same conclusion from a completely different, payroll-based methodology: "no decisive evidence" of a takeoff. Three independent Tier-1 sources — Syverson's critique, BLS's actual data, and Stanford's composite indicators — all cut against treating a single market-based estimate as settled fact, however methodologically clever it is.
