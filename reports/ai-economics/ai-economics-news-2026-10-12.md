## What capex implies: AI-sector productivity must rise ~2.7x (Wachter & Wachter, NBER w35290)

`capex` `productivity` `gdp` `academic` `nber` `forecast` · **Source:** [NBER w35290, "What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290) · *Found: 2026-10-12*

**Tier 1 (NBER working paper; theory and calibration, not causal identification).** Jessica Wachter (Wharton) and Jonathan Wachter (Point72) note that the five largest US tech firms spent ~$380B in capex in 2025, with roughly double forecast for 2026. In a two-sector open-economy model with rare productivity booms, they assume marginal investment has zero NPV and find that spending is rational only if AI-sector productivity rises by ~2.7x. Scenarios yield 5 to 58 percentage points of additional cumulative GDP by 2030 (AI at 8% to 39% of the economy), expected long-run growth ~7% with substantial risk, a ~0.5pp higher risk-free rate and a ~3pp higher equity premium. The paper issued in June 2026; Wharton Knowledge's [Sept. 1 write-up](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/can-ai-productivity-grow-fast-enough-to-justify-big-techs-spending/) is where it got attention.

Reading it: this is an *inversion* (what must be true), not a forecast. Wachter calls 2.7x "an eye-popping number" but argues it is plausible given current valuations, and sees little resemblance to dot-com (a capex boom, not just a stock-price boom). The authors themselves concede the spending "may simply reflect a bubble," that the boom shows up "in investment data but not yet in productivity data," and that failure would be "the largest misallocation of capital in history." Note the 5–58pp range is a level shift relative to baseline, so its width is the real message: the model barely constrains the outcome.

## Contrarian Take

### What capex implies: AI-sector productivity must rise ~2.7x
The Wachters' inversion treats current spending as the revealed belief of rational investors and asks what world justifies it. The counter-case is that capex is not a clean revealed-preference signal: Morgan Stanley's financing analysis (Tier 2, Feb 2026) shows capex-to-sales for the big five at 34–39% including only owned assets, and 38–45% with financing leases, above the ~32% internet-era peak, while Moody's (Tier 2) flags $662B of off-balance-sheet lease commitments and eroding free cash flow. Spending funded by debt and by circular counterparties (e.g. the Oracle backlog contingent on OpenAI's cash burn, covered 2026-10-05) can persist well past the point where marginal NPV turns negative, which is exactly how the 1990s fiber overbuild happened, and the paper itself cites that precedent. Also, Census BTOS random-sample adoption (~19.8%) and the Fed's 1.4% q/q Q2 productivity print are far from a 2.7x jump. The paper's own admission that gains appear "in investment data but not yet in productivity data" is the Solow paradox restated with a price tag.
